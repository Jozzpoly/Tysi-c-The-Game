# Interaction Aesthetics and Skill

## Why this document exists

Truthful, legible interaction can still feel sterile.

This project therefore needs a second axis beyond correctness and causality: **the aesthetic quality of action itself**.

The goal is not decorative beauty layered over usable controls. The goal is interaction whose movement, resistance, timing, rhythm and response are themselves pleasurable, expressive and capable of supporting skill.

## 1. Appearance and action both carry meaning

Traditional visual design often treats aesthetics as form, color, texture and composition.

Interaction design adds another material:

- pickup;
- movement;
- resistance;
- rhythm;
- release;
- recovery;
- response;
- coordination with other objects.

A card can look beautiful and feel dead.

A visually simple card can feel excellent if its action language is coherent and expressive.

## 2. Interaction aesthetics is not "juice"

Juice/amplification emphasizes events through extra sensory feedback.

Interaction aesthetics is broader.

It includes:

- how smoothly control enters the body/tool loop;
- whether motion has rhythm;
- whether resistance feels meaningful;
- whether repeated actions develop fluency;
- whether users can express subtle intent;
- whether action and response have a distinctive character.

A completely unjuiced manipulation can still have strong interaction aesthetics.

## 3. Skill is a design target

Many interfaces optimize for immediate ease and remove nearly all need for learning.

That is appropriate for many tasks but not necessarily for long-lived creative or game systems.

A rich interaction may deliberately leave room for:

- timing skill;
- spatial precision;
- fluent reordering;
- fast acquisition;
- deliberate release;
- efficient grouping;
- expert shortcuts;
- personal style.

The challenge is to create **learnable richness**, not arbitrary difficulty.

## 4. Easy is not the only good

An interaction that requires zero motor learning may also offer zero mastery.

Do not equate:

- no challenge with good UX;
- automation with support;
- snapping everything with precision;
- reducing all action to clicks with efficiency.

Research should distinguish:

- accidental difficulty;
- arbitrary difficulty;
- meaningful motor/cognitive skill;
- expressive possibility.

## 5. Expression

A strong object may allow different users to achieve the same semantic action with subtly different movement qualities.

Potential examples for future card research:

- slow deliberate lift;
- fast reorder flick;
- controlled throw toward play zone;
- tiny adjustment inside hand;
- grouping through spacing.

The system does not need to reward style mechanically.

But it may allow style to exist without collapsing every gesture into identical button activation.

## 6. Character

Interaction can have character without becoming theatrical.

Character may emerge from a coherent relationship among:

- damping;
- acceleration;
- rotation;
- spacing;
- sound material;
- settle behavior;
- visual thickness;
- target acquisition;
- timing.

This is closer to designing a material or instrument than choosing an animation preset.

## 7. Rhythm

Repeated interaction creates temporal patterns.

In Tysiac, likely rhythms include:

- browse hand;
- acquire card;
- manipulate/aim;
- commit;
- opponent response;
- trick resolution;
- regain agency.

Good rhythm depends on alternation:

- tension / release;
- action / observation;
- local control / shared consequence;
- quiet / impact.

A constant 250 ms easing curve across every event cannot create this.

## 8. Motor fluency

A candidate interaction should be tested for whether users become less consciously dependent on visual checking over time.

Potential evidence:

- faster manipulation with stable error rate;
- fewer corrective submovements;
- less hesitation at commit boundary;
- stable personal gesture patterns;
- increasing use of workspace organization.

This may indicate the interaction is becoming incorporated into skilled practice.

## 9. Richness vs unpredictability

Rich interaction needs more degrees of expression, but too many uncontrolled degrees create noise.

Examples:

- grab-point rotation can increase expressiveness;
- unconstrained 3D tumble can destroy readability;
- release velocity can feel good;
- excessive inertia can make settle unpredictable.

Design target:

**rich enough to support skill, constrained enough to support confidence.**

## 10. Sensory aesthetics

Sound and haptic feedback can contribute to material character, but should remain causally tied to action.

Potential semantic distinctions:

- contact;
- lift;
- slide/reorder;
- threshold/commit;
- accepted placement;
- rejection;
- capture/collection;
- high-significance resolution.

Do not use a generic click family for everything.

Do not use haptics to compensate for unclear visuals.

## 11. Aesthetic fit

The same movement quality can be excellent or wrong depending on intended experiential quality.

Examples:

- elastic bounce may feel playful in one context and cheap in another;
- heavy damping may feel substantial or sluggish;
- rapid snap may feel precise or sterile;
- large follow-through may feel expressive or imprecise.

Therefore interaction attributes cannot be judged without asking:

> what experiential quality is this interaction supposed to carry?

For Tysiac we have not yet fixed that quality.

This is deliberate.

## 12. Avoid premature style labels

Do not begin with:

- premium;
- tactile;
- juicy;
- elegant;
- physical;
- arcade;
- serious.

These adjectives are too broad and bias implementation.

First identify observable interaction qualities and Owner reactions.

Then synthesize a character.

## 13. Skill and accessibility

Skill headroom must coexist with accessible alternatives.

A primary gesture can be rich while equivalent semantic actions remain available through:

- keyboard;
- switch control;
- simplified interaction mode;
- explicit selection/commit controls;
- reduced motion.

Accessibility should preserve semantic power even if the embodied path differs.

## 14. Transfer beyond cards

### Jozz Vehicle

Component manipulation should become something the Owner can learn bodily: grabbing, stretching, pivoting, snapping and topology editing can have expressive fluency.

### JES

World/matter tools can develop characteristic control feel that communicates material/constraint differences without forcing every operation through numeric panels.

### Multi World

Object manipulation can support mastery in pickup, throw, rotation and placement rather than treating physics only as simulation output.

### LLM Live NPC

Less directly motor, but still relevant: inspection/debug interaction can become fluent and spatial rather than modal/dashboard-heavy.

## 15. Failure conditions

Reject an aesthetically rich interaction if:

- it masks incorrect system truth;
- it adds motor difficulty with no expressive value;
- it becomes exhausting under repetition;
- its skill is arbitrary memorization rather than embodied fluency;
- style interferes with comprehension;
- the user must fight the object's personality to perform precise intent;
- character is inconsistent across related actions.

## 16. Research requirement

Future Owner tests should not only ask:

- was it clear?
- was it fast?
- did it work?

They should observe:

- did the Owner keep touching it without external goal?
- did movement become more fluent during the session?
- did the Owner discover expressive actions not instructed by us?
- did the interaction invite experimentation?
- did repetition increase attachment or irritation?
- did the system feel more controllable as skill increased?

A donor-quality interaction should ideally become **better as the user becomes better at using it**.
